Coding Guide Video

Already know one language and want to learn the next one? In this 6-minute video ThePrimeagen explains his three-step method for learning a new programming language – a perfect companion to the comparison table above.

Video text transcript

Full text of the video. Descriptions of what is on screen are in italics.

On screen (0:00): ThePrimeagen sits at his desk wearing headphones, with a microphone in front of him and a virtual office background with a red “TheStartup” logo. He talks straight to the camera and gestures a lot with his hands. Whenever he shows his screen, his webcam picture stays small in the bottom-right corner.

0:00 Intro

0:00 “So I've learned quite a few different languages through my 20 years as an expert HTML programmer, and during these 20 years I've kind of developed a road map to learn new languages, and I wanted to take the time to show you how I do that. Because when I first started out I didn't really have a direction, but as I started learning and getting more proficient I kind of developed my own little bit of a pattern.”

0:23 “Now the thing is, this video is strictly meant for someone not learning their first language. This is really a second-and-beyond language, and not all languages will really work this way. I can imagine that if you're learning JavaScript and you want to learn Haskell, this pattern may not 100% line up, just because Haskell is such a different language from JavaScript.”

0:46 Step 1: Exploration / documentation

0:46 “So the first of three steps that I have is the exploration slash documentation phase. Now this may take you a little bit depending on your programming chops, but it's a really simple process. Practically speaking, what that means is going to Advent of Code and starting to solve problems.”

0:57 On screen: his web browser with the Advent of Code 2022 puzzle “Day 1: Calorie Counting”. He scrolls down to the example input, a list of numbers, which is highlighted.

1:03 “Why I choose this is a couple of reasons. First off, you'll notice right here that this should be in a file – you have to read from a file system. File reading can fail, therefore there's going to be errors, so it gives me a chance to understand how errors work in the system, how string processing works, if, while, iterator structures, enums. It just gives me a good overview of the language and what tools are really available for me to solve problems in it.”

1:25 “It also gives me a chance to look at how they handle null and undefined, because in Rust it's going to be an Option, whereas in TypeScript it's going to be null or undefined, and so they're very different ways you handle it – pattern matching, if statements – it's totally different. It gives you a good overview, and every time you run into something you don't understand, you go look it up in the documentation and read about it. That way you just learn by doing.”

1:49 “I think that's a very important point. I am not somebody – and I doubt you are either – who can just read through pages upon pages of what the language does and then, boom, I can program because I'm amazing at it. That's usually not how it works. Usually we learn with our hands more than with our eyes; we have to program through a bunch of problems to pick everything up.”

2:14 On screen: the Advent of Code 2022 calendar page with its ASCII-art picture and the stars he has collected. “The second reason why I really love Advent of Code – which I strongly suggest you do – is that there's a bunch of problems here. I only go so far in Advent of Code; I don't like to go all the way up, it gets very tedious. But tons of people have either solved the problem or are going to solve it with you, so you can ask questions, bounce answers off of people, code review other people's solutions. People write blogs about it. There's a lot of available information about solving these problems specifically in the language you're looking for, so you get a bunch of experience right away.”

2:45 Step 2: A websocket server

2:45 At 2:48 the video goes back to the camera. “The second one I like to do is a websocket server. I think this is super important, and I really enjoy this phase of the learning process, because I usually build something super simple: a chat room that takes connections. Someone says ‘join’ and a string, someone says ‘leave’ and the string, or they send ‘message’, the name of the room and the message they want to send to everyone in that room.”

3:08 “It gives me the ability to hold on to sockets. How do we do sockets? What does the standard library actually provide versus what third-party libraries are out there? It really gives you a good idea of what the ecosystem is, how modules work in the language, and the best part: how does it handle async? Are there primitives around it, or do you just have to use threads? How do you actually work with async in the language?”

3:31 Step 3: A larger project

3:31 “My third goal is usually to build a larger project – something that makes me exercise a lot of patterns and has to maintain itself with lots of lines of code, because often you'll start finding weird chinks in the armor: how memory is stored, how the borrow checker works, how bad your ideas around it are. It has to get to a certain level before you can really feel the language out.”

3:58 At 4:06, for a few seconds, the screen shows the website of the book “Writing An Interpreter In Go” by Thorsten Ball, then the camera returns. “For my recent endeavors into Zig I've chosen to build an interpreter – this interpreter right here, fantastic book, Thorsten Ball. Why I chose that is that I'm going to build the interpreter in TypeScript, then in Rust and then finally in Zig, so I can use the experience from languages I'm good at in a language I'm bad at, and draw similarities and differences in how Zig handles various things.”

4:22 From 4:29 the screen shows his TypeScript Tokenizer class (index.ts) in the Neovim editor: a long switch statement that creates a token for each character, followed by if checks for letters and keywords. “Let me give you a good example. If you're familiar with TypeScript, building a lexer means you come across all these tokens and you have to create these little tokens. I define the token, and based on all these different conditions I return a token, and at the end I also have to check for the more programmatic cases, because there's no way to express it all in one statement unless you only use ifs and elses.”

4:47 On screen: the same lexer in Rust (lexer.rs). He highlights the match self.ch branch for letters and underscores, which reads an identifier and checks for keywords such as fn, let and if. “Whereas with Rust you can do a match statement, in which I can express all these different things simply in one statement. Look at this: if it matches this, I go through my identifier process – whereas in TypeScript that's just a function doing the exact same thing.”

5:05 On screen: the lexer in Zig (lexer.zig), with a very similar switch branch for the letters a–z, A–Z and the underscore highlighted. “And the cool part is Zig actually has something very similar. You'll notice right here I can do almost the same thing I do in Rust, so I get the cool parts of Rust and more of the ease of handling of TypeScript – a really interesting middle-ground language.”

5:21 At 5:23 the video goes back to the camera for the rest of the video. “Usually by the time I'm done with the project I have a pretty strong grasp. For me to really know if I like a language, I have to build a few things. If you don't build a few things it's really hard to know how the language actually feels. A lot of ideas seem great on paper, but until you build something sufficiently large they're just ideas – they're not concrete, you don't know if they're good, they're just whiteboard.”

5:47 Plans and the “fourth step”

5:47 “So that's how I go about learning languages. I'm going to apply this exact same principle soon on Twitch with OCaml, once I'm done building the interpreter in these three languages, and see if I can keep applying it even in languages very far outside the ones I know – OCaml being closest to Rust, but a very functional, very different language. My guess is it should not be that hard. Pretty dang excited about it.”

6:10 He leans close to the camera and grins. “Oh, I actually forgot the fourth step. The fourth step, of course, is to press like and subscribe. It just works, it makes you better. I don't know why. The name is ThePrimeagen.”
